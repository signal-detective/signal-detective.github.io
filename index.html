Pandemic Signal Detective

Which kind of data warns us first?

When a wave of disease starts, nobody announces it. You have to spot it in the data. Six different sources were tracking the same pandemic, and each one catches a person at a different stage of getting sick.

infected, then Google your symptoms, then tell a survey you feel sick, then see a doctor, then test positive, then go to hospital, and some people die

So some of them should move earlier than others. These are real numbers from all 50 US states and the District of Columbia, 51 places in total, between June 2020 and March 2022. Find out which signal warns us first, then build an alarm with it.

2Does it hold up everywhere?

One state could be luck, so this runs the same comparison on all 50 states and DC. For each one we slide a signal backwards and forwards in time until it best matches that state's case curve. How far we had to slide it is its warning time.

Each dot is one of the 51 places. Dots right of the zero line warned early, which epidemiologists call a leading indicator. Dots left of it moved after cases, a lagging indicator. The thick black line is the median: line the places up in order and it is the middle one, which is a fairer summary than any single place because one odd result cannot drag it far.

3Watch a wave cross the country

Drag the slider or press play. Try an early signal, then a late one, and watch which lights up first.

4Build an alarm

Now make something useful out of it. Your alarm watches the signals you tick and goes off when they climb far enough above where they started and stay there.

Two dials. Level is how big a jump you demand: 1.5 means the signals have climbed to one and a half times their baseline, the level they sat at during the first week shown. Days in a row is how long that has to last before you believe it.

You are trying to beat the surge, marked on the lower chart. That is the day cases reached half their peak, the point where a wave is unmistakably underway. Loose settings ring early but cry wolf. Strict settings are trustworthy but slow. No setting wins on both, which is the whole problem.

Worth discussing

  1. Searches has no data at all for eight of the 51 places, and the survey only reached people on Facebook. Cases only counted people who got a test. Whose illness does each source miss?
  2. In 2013 Google Flu Trends badly overshot, partly because news coverage sent healthy people searching for symptoms. Which of these six signals could break the same way, and when?
  3. This data came from millions of people's searches, surveys and medical records, anonymised and pooled. What would you want to be true before your own data was used this way?

Data from the Delphi Epidata API at Carnegie Mellon, which gathered these signals from Google, Facebook, health systems, Johns Hopkins and the US Department of Health and Human Services. The assembled file and a Colab notebook version are in the repository.
